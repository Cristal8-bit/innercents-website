Money & Values Quiz

Question 1: You receive $50 as a gift. What matters most to you when deciding how to use it?

Saving it all for a future goal
Sharing some with family or friends who might need it
Spending it on something fun for myself
Investing it to grow over time
💭 Reflection: There's no wrong answer here! This reveals your core values. Do you value security, generosity, joy, or growth? Understanding your values helps you make financial decisions that feel right.

Insight: Your answer reflects your personal values around money. Someone who values community might naturally share, while someone focused on security might save. Both are valid—the key is knowing yourself!

Question 2: When you think about money, what emotion comes up first?

Excitement and possibility
Stress and worry
Guilt or shame
Confusion or uncertainty
💭 Reflection: Our emotions about money are shaped by our experiences and what we've learned from family and culture. Recognizing these feelings is the first step to a healthier relationship with finances.

Insight: All these emotions are common and valid! Many people feel stress about money because of systemic inequities or past experiences. Acknowledging your feelings helps you address them constructively.

Question 3: Your friend wants to borrow money from you. What's your first consideration?

Can I afford to lend this without affecting my own needs?
Will they pay me back on time?
How can I help them in a way that preserves our friendship?
Is this enabling unhealthy financial behavior?
💭 Reflection: Money and relationships are complex! This question explores how you balance helping others with protecting yourself financially and emotionally.

Insight: The "best" answer considers both practical finances AND the relationship. You can care about someone while also setting healthy boundaries. Sometimes the most caring response is helping them find other resources.

Your Results

Remember: This quiz is about self-awareness, not right or wrong answers. Understanding your values and emotional responses to money helps you make financial decisions that align with who you are and what matters most to you.

Emotional Intelligence & Money Quiz

Question 1: You just made an impulse purchase you regret. How do you respond?

Beat myself up about it and promise never to do it again
Acknowledge the feeling, understand what triggered it, and plan differently
Ignore it and move on—everyone makes mistakes
Return the item immediately, no matter what
💭 Reflection: How we respond to financial mistakes reveals our emotional maturity. Self-compassion combined with learning is more effective than shame or avoidance.

Insight: Option B shows emotional intelligence! It combines self-compassion with accountability. Understanding WHY we made the purchase (stress? boredom? peer pressure?) helps prevent future regrets.

Question 2: A family member criticizes your spending habits. How do you feel and respond?

Defensive—they don't understand my situation
Ashamed—maybe they're right to judge me
Curious—what can I learn, even if the delivery was harsh?
Angry—my money is none of their business
💭 Reflection: Money conversations with family can be emotionally charged. How we navigate criticism reveals our ability to separate emotions from useful feedback.

Insight: While all reactions are understandable, curiosity helps us grow! We can acknowledge our feelings (defensiveness, shame, anger) while still asking: "Is there wisdom here I can use?" It doesn't mean the criticism was fair—just that we're emotionally mature enough to extract value.

Question 3: You see friends enjoying things you can't afford right now. What helps you most?

Remembering my own values and goals—I'm on my own journey
Finding free or low-cost ways to connect with them
Avoiding situations where I feel "less than"
Working extra hours to keep up financially
💭 Reflection: Social comparison around money is natural but can be painful. Emotional intelligence means recognizing these feelings and responding in healthy ways.

Insight: Options A and B both show emotional intelligence! A focuses on internal values (reducing comparison), while B maintains connections creatively. C might protect you short-term but could increase isolation. D risks burnout and keeps you stuck in comparison mode.

Your Emotional Intelligence Score

Emotional intelligence with money is a skill you can develop! The more you practice recognizing your feelings, understanding what triggers them, and responding thoughtfully, the stronger this skill becomes.

Practical Financial Skills Quiz

Question 1: What is the 50/30/20 budgeting rule?

Save 50%, spend 30%, invest 20%
50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings
50% housing, 30% food, 20% other
Spend 50 days planning, 30 days acting, 20 days reviewing
💭 Reflection: Budgeting rules are guidelines, not rigid laws. Different life circumstances require different approaches. What matters is finding a system that works for YOUR life.

Correct! The 50/30/20 rule suggests 50% for needs (housing, food, utilities), 30% for wants (entertainment, dining out), and 20% for savings and debt repayment. But remember—if you're in a high cost-of-living area or facing systemic barriers, these percentages might not be realistic. Adapt the concept to your reality!

Question 2: What does it mean to "pay yourself first"?

Spend money on things you enjoy before paying bills
Always withdraw cash before using cards
Put money into savings before spending on anything else
Prioritize your salary over side income
💭 Reflection: This concept recognizes that if we wait to save "what's left over," there often isn't anything left. It's about prioritizing your future self—an act of self-care!

Correct! "Pay yourself first" means setting aside savings as soon as you get income, treating it like a non-negotiable bill to your future self. This ensures you're building wealth and security, not just surviving paycheck to paycheck.

Question 3: What is a credit score primarily used for?

To determine how trustworthy you are with borrowed money
To calculate how much money you have in the bank
To track your investment portfolio performance
To determine your annual salary
💭 Reflection: Credit scores can feel like a judgment of your worth, but they're really just a tool lenders use. Understanding how they work helps you use them strategically, not let them define you.

Correct! Your credit score (usually 300-850) shows lenders how reliably you've managed credit in the past. Higher scores can get you better interest rates on loans. But remember: having a lower score doesn't make you a bad person—it just means you need to rebuild that trust with lenders, often after facing systemic barriers or life challenges.
